On paper, personal finance software, household tools, and archaeology-focused products have almost nothing to do with each other.

In practice, the product questions underneath are often the same. That is probably why I enjoy moving between them.

## The domain changes, the judgement does not

The language, users, and constraints change. The useful questions stay familiar.

I keep coming back to things like:

- what problem is painful enough to matter?
- what can be simplified without becoming shallow?
- where should the interface lead, and where should it get out of the way?
- how do you let the system grow without making it harder to understand?

Those questions travel well.

## Rich domains expose weak product thinking quickly

A domain with more texture can actually help because it makes lazy decisions obvious.

In finance, people notice quickly if the product creates confusion instead of clarity. In household software, they notice if the workflow is annoying. In archaeology or education products, they notice if visual ambition starts getting in the way of comprehension.

Different surface area, same test: does the software help someone do the thing more clearly?

That consistency is one of the reasons I do not worry much about the domains looking mismatched from the outside. The surface changes. The judgement work feels surprisingly stable.
