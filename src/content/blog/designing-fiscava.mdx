Fiscava got more interesting the moment I stopped treating it like an expense tracker.

Recording transactions is table stakes. Plenty of products can do that. The harder problem, and the one I care about more, is helping someone understand what their money is doing and what they should probably pay attention to next.

That sounds like a small shift. It is not. It changes the shape of the whole product.

## Expense trackers hit a ceiling quickly

Simple trackers are useful for capture. After that, a lot of them flatten out.

Once someone wants better context, clearer summaries, or a stronger sense of progress, the product has to move beyond categories and charts. The job stops being "log the activity" and becomes "help me see the situation clearly enough to act".

## More features is usually the lazy answer

The easy move is to add more charts, more categories, more breakdowns, more settings.

Most of the time that is just complexity wearing a respectable outfit. The better question is which decision the product is meant to support, then shaping the model around that instead of rewarding yourself for surface area.

That has been the more useful direction for Fiscava. Not bigger for the sake of it. Broader only where the product genuinely needs a better view of the person's financial position.

## The hard part is product judgement

Finance software gets bloated quickly because almost every feature idea sounds reasonable in isolation.

The difficult bit is deciding what deserves to be first class, what can stay implied, and what should be cut before the whole product turns into homework. That is where most of the real design work has been.

I still like the technical side, obviously. But the thing that keeps me interested is the judgement. A finance product is basically a long series of decisions about what to clarify, what to leave alone, and how much complexity a person should be asked to carry.
