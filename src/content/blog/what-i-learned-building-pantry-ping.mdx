Household software looks trivial right up until you try to make it genuinely useful.

A shopping list sounds simple. Then you run into repeat purchases, pantry state, duplicate items, recurring staples, and the awkward fact that the app is supposed to reduce domestic friction rather than become another tiny admin system people resent.

## The work was in the workflow

The interesting part of pantry-ping was never the list itself.

The real work sat in the relationship between list items, pantry stock, and recurring needs. Once those stop being separate features and start being treated as one workflow, the product questions get better.

You stop asking, "How do I add another capability?" and start asking, "What should happen next so nobody has to think about this twice?"

## Good defaults matter more than flashy features

In a household tool, every extra tap is expensive because the app only earns its place by being used over and over.

That pushed the design towards strong defaults, predictable state changes, and behaviour that feels boring in the best possible way. Nobody wants to admire their shopping-list app. They want it to quietly stop them forgetting dishwashing tablets again.

## Edge cases are where trust is won

Projects like this keep reminding me that edge cases are often the product.

Duplicate handling, recurrence rules, stock transitions, linked pantry items: those are not implementation leftovers. They are the points where people either trust the app or mentally demote it back to "just a list".

If the behaviour is slightly off, people feel it immediately. And in household software, slightly off is enough to get you ignored.
